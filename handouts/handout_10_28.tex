\section{Numerals}
\subsection{The syntactic position of numerals}
As shown by \textcite{bicevskisQuantificationGitksan2017}, Gitksan numerals can appear either preverbally or postverbally. This is confirmed by our elicitations as well.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 
    \begin{xlist}
    \item     \label{ex:postgilbil} \glll gya'ayhl gilbilhl smex \\
    gya'a-y=hl gilbil=hl smex \\
    see-\Fsg{}.\Ii{}=\Cn{} two=\Cn{} bear \\
    `I saw two bears ' \hfill HH.2026.10.01
    \item \label{ex:pregilbil} \glll gilbil smex gya'ay \\
    gilbil smex gya'a-y \\
    two bear see-\Fsg{}.\Ii{} \\
    `I saw two bears ' \hfill HH.2026.10.01
    \end{xlist}
\end{exe}

According to \textcite{bicevskisQuantificationGitksan2017}, weak quantifiers also pattern similarly, where they can appear preverbally or postverbally with \textit{-a}, the \Attr{} suffix. We find that HH translates weak quantifiers preferentially as preverbal.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 
    \begin{xlist}
    \item    \glll ? gya'a'yhl hilda smex \\
    {} gya'a-'y=hl hild-a smex \\
    {} see-\Fsg{}.\Ii{}=\Cn{} many-\Attr{} bear\\
    Intended: `I saw many bears.'\hfill HH.2026.10.01
    \item \glll hilthl smex gya'a'y \\
    hilt=hl smex gya'a-'y \\
    many=\Cn{} bear see-\Fsg{}.\Ii{}\\
    `I saw many bears.' \hfill HH.2026.10.01
    \end{xlist}
\end{exe}

This may not be an accident. For many instances of postverbal weak quantifiers, Hector explains that there needs to be some sort of supporting context.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 
    \begin{xlist}
    \item   \glll
    gya'a'y hilda hun \\
    gya'a'y[=hl?] hild-a hun \\
    see-\Fsg.\Ii[=\Cn?] many-\Attr{} fish \\
    \glt `I saw many fish.' \hfill HH.2026.10.01 \\
    \textbf{Comment:} "Answers a question ... somewhere where there’s no fish, no water. ... You’re telling a story... Might be surprising." [Later says, there of course has to be water.]
    \item \glll 
    gya'a'y hild-a hun g̱a'asun \\
    gya'a'y hild-a hun  g̱a'a[-t]=t=xʷin \\
    see-\Fsg.\I{} many-\Attr{} fish \Loc[3.\Ii]=\Pn=\Dem.\Prox \\
    \glt `I saw many fish over [there/here].' \hfill HH.2026.10.01 \\
    \textbf{Comment:} If we're all there and all saw them, it's like you're bragging, taking credit. 
    \item \glll
    gya'a'y hilda hun duu'w \\
    gya'a'y hild-a hun  duu'w \\
    see-\Fsg.\Ii[=\Cn?] many-\Attr{} fish over.there \\
    \glt `I saw many fish (way) over there.'  \hfill HH.2026.10.01 \\
    \textbf{Comment:} "That means you were over there; you're not seeing it now; or you're over here and you still see it"
    \end{xlist}
\end{exe}

In fact, the same is true for (\ref{ex:postgilbil}), repeated below. HH says that this conveys some form of ``excitment'', and he adds phonological stress on the predicate when repeating the sentence.


\begin{exe}
    \ex \glll gya'ayhl gilbilhl smex \\
    gya'a-y=hl gilbil=hl smex \\
    see-\Fsg{}.\Ii{}=\Cn{} two=\Cn{} bear \\
    `I saw two bears ' \hfill  HH.2026.10.01\\
\end{exe}

We take it that these comments are not accidental. For both weak quantifiers and numerals, postverbal uses require some form of predicate-focus, whereas preverbal uses are unmarked.



\subsection{Enter \textit{\tsb{g}abii}} 
As per \textcite[310]{bicevskisQuantificationGitksan2017}, numerals can co-occur with the ``count amount'' (\Cnt{})  morpheme \textit{\tsb{g}abii}, where ``sentences are truth-conditionally equivalent to their alternants without
\textit{\tsb{g}abi}''. Note that this is the same \textit{\tsb{g}abii} that occurs when questioning an amount.\footnote{There is also a way to ask for amount using \textit{da'mis/dimis}, e.g.: 
\begin{exe}
    \ex \glll dimishl \tsb{g}abii duus sdem\tsb{k}it \\
    ?=hl \tsb{g}abii duus sdam\tsb{k}-it \\
    how.many? \Cnt{} cat make.noise-\Sx \\
    `How many cats meowed?' 
\end{exe}
We do not discuss it here, but the difference between \textit{dimis} and \textit{\tsb{g}abii} remains unclear.}

\begin{exe}
    \ex \glll hindehl \tsb{g}abiihl smex gya'an \\
    hinde=hl \tsb{g}abii=hl smex gya'a-n \\
    \Wh{}=\Cn{} \Cnt{}=\Cn{} bear see-\Ssg{}.\Ii{} \\
    `How many bears did you see?'
\end{exe}

We find, however, that numerals with and without \textit{\tsb{g}abii} are not supported by the same contexts, neither syntactically nor semantically. 

First of all, in my best attempt at eliciting an unambiguously predicative numeral, it seems that \textit{\tsb{g}abii} is required. Weak quantifier \textit{hilt} is included as a minimlar pair. 

\begin{exe}
    \ex 
    \begin{xlist}
       \item \textbf{Context: }Your friend invited your family to a party, and he asks who will come. You don't know how many family members will come, but you don't want to lowball it.      
       \glll dim hilt 'nuu'm \\
    dim hilt 'nuu'm \\
    \Prosp{} many \Fpl{}.\Iii{} \\
    `We will be many.' \textit{VF}: `There's gonna be a lot of us.'
        \item \glll ?? dim xwsdins 'nuu'm \\
      {} dim xwsdins \tsb{g}abii-'m \\
     {} \Prosp{} five \Fpl{}.\Iii{} \\
    \textit{Intended: }`We will be five.'
    \item \glll dim xwsdins \tsb{g}abii'm \\
     dim xwsdins \tsb{g}abii-'m \\
    \Prosp{} five \Cnt{}-\Fpl{}.\Ii{} \\
    `We will be five.'
    \end{xlist}
\end{exe}

Second, in contexts where an ``at least'' reading is intended, we cannot have \textit{\tsb{g}abii}. Compare the following:

\begin{exe}
    \ex 
    \begin{xlist}
        \item \glll gwlunhl hlk'iinaa \\
    gwlun=hl hlk'ii-n=aa \\
    three=\Cn{} have.children-\Ssg{}.\Ii{}=\Q{} \\
    `Do you have three children?' \hfill HH.2026.10.15
       \item \textbf{Context:} A surveyor comes to your door to make note of households that have 3 children or more for some government benefit. If the surveyor asks ``do you have three children?'', but you have five children. \\
        \glll gwlunhl (*\tsb{g}abiihl) hlk'ii'y, iin k'ap xwsdins \tsb{g}abiihl hlk'ii  \\
    gwlun=hl hlk'ii-'y, ii=n k'ap xwsdins \tsb{g}abii=hl hlk'ii  \\
    three=\Cn{} have.children-\Fsg{}.\Ii{}, \Ccnj{}=\Fsg{}.\I{} \Verum{} five=\Cn{} \Cnt{}=\Cn{} have.children \\
    `I have three children, and actually I have five children (in total).' \hfill HH.2026.10.15
    \item \textbf{Context:} A surveyor comes to your door, and in this case you have to have three childeren to qualifiy for the policy. You do have three children. \\
        \glll ee, gwlunhl (\tsb{g}abiihl) hlk'ii'y \\
    ee, gwlun=hl \tsb{g}abii=hl hlk'ii-'y \\
    yes, three=\Cn{} \Cnt{}=\Cn{} have.children-\Fsg{}.\Ii{} \\
    `Yes, I have three children (in total).' \hfill HH.2026.10.15
    \end{xlist}
\end{exe}

Therefore, numerals with and without \textit{\tsb{g}abii} are different both syntactically and semantically. Syntactically, it seems that \textit{\tsb{g}abii} is necessary for a predicative numeral, while semantically, \textit{\tsb{g}abii} results in an ``exact amount'' reading.

One more qualification here, however, is that numerals with \textit{\tsb{g}abii} are not required to be predicative. The following tests an unambiguously moved NumP \parencite{forbesAdjectives2012}, and we see that HH still requires \textit{\tsb{g}abii} in this context.

\begin{exe}
    \ex \glll xbil *(\tsb{g}abiihl) smexhl \tsb{g}at'uuts'xwit \\
    xbil \tsb{g}abii=hl smex=hl \tsb{g}a-t'uuts'xw-it \\
    ten  \Cnt{}=\Cn{} bear=\Cn{} \Distr{}-black-\Sx{}   \\
    `There are ten black bears.' \hfill HH.2026.10.08
\end{exe}

What to make of the fact that \textit{\tsb{g}abii} is seemingly composed using the distributive prefix \textit{\tsb{g}a-} \parencite[368]{bicevskisQuantificationGitksan2017}? We are not yet sure, but it certainly presents an interesting puzzle.

Also, we have not tackled \textit{\tsb{g}as\tsb{g}oo} as systematically.
